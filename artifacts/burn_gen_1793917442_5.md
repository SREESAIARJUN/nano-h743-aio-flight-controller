# STM32H743 AIO Flight Controller + 4-in-1 ESC: Design Reference (Job #0)

## 0. Scope and Assumptions

### 0.1 System architecture

An STM32H743 does not commutate motors on a 4-in-1 ESC. The realistic architecture is an all-in-one (AIO) board:

- **Flight controller domain:** STM32H743 running ArduPilot. It generates four bidirectional DShot600 streams.
- **ESC domain:** four small MCUs (AT32F421 or STM32G071 running AM32, or EFM8/AT32 running BLHeli_32-class firmware). Each drives one 3-phase bridge of 6 MOSFETs.

I model both domains. Every number below follows from a stated parameter, so you can substitute your actual BOM.

### 0.3 Feasibility flag

80 A *per motor* on a nano-format board is only physically achievable as a burst of a few seconds. Section 1.6 derives this limit. Many "80A" nano ESC ratings are total-current or sub-second marketing figures. The design must enforce the limit in firmware, not assume continuous capability.

## 1. Thermal and Loss Model

### 1.1 Temperature-dependent on-resistance

$$R_{ds}(T_j) = R_{ds,25}\,[1 + \alpha_R (T_j - 25)]$$

At T_j = 110 °C: R = 1.5 mΩ × 1.51 = **2.27 mΩ**.

Because R_ds(on) rises with temperature, conduction loss and junction temperature are coupled. Section 1.5 solves this iteratively.

### 1.2 Conduction loss: 6-step, synchronous rectification, complementary PWM

At any instant one phase is PWM-switched (high-side for fraction D, synchronous low-side for 1−D) and one phase's low-side is fully on. Current I therefore always flows through exactly two channel resistances:

$$P_{cond,motor} = I^2 R_{ds}(T_j)\,[D + (1-D)] + I^2 R_{ds}(T_j) = 2 I^2 R_{ds}(T_j)$$

This is independent of duty cycle, which is a useful property of synchronous complementary drive. The relation also holds for sinusoidal FOC using RMS phase current: $P = 3 I_{ph,rms}^2 R_{ds}$, with $I_{ph,rms} = I_{pk}/\sqrt2$.

At 80 A and 110 °C:

$$P_{cond} = 2 \times 6400 \times 2.27\,\text{m}\Omega = \mathbf{29.1\ W\ per\ motor}$$

**Per-FET distribution.** The electrical frequency is much higher than the thermal bandwidth: a 14-pole motor at 25 k RPM gives f_e ≈ 2.9 kHz, while τ_JC is about 10 ms. Each FET therefore sees time-averaged loss. Each phase conducts 2/3 of the electrical cycle, split between its high-side and low-side, so:

$$\bar P_{FET} \approx P_{cond}/6 = 4.85\ \text{W}$$

The worst-case FET is the low-side at low duty. Its peak is ~4.85 W × 1.5 ≈ 7.3 W.

**Copper interconnect loss** (often forgotten). Assume a 20 mm path from battery pad to bridge, 4 layers of 2 oz, 5 mm wide:

$$R_{Cu} = \rho\frac{L}{A} = 1.72\times10^{-8}\cdot\frac{0.02}{4\cdot70\,\mu\text{m}\cdot5\,\text{mm}} \approx 0.25\ \text{m}\Omega \;(\times1.3\text{ at }100\ °\text{C})$$

For the shared 320 A DC bus this gives P_Cu ≈ 320² × 0.32 mΩ ≈ **33 W**. Bus copper is a first-order heat source and must be distributed. Run the battery input as a pad-to-bridge star; never daisy-chain it.

### 1.3 Switching loss at 48 kHz

| Term | Formula | Value per motor |
|---|---|---|
| V–I overlap | ½·V·I·(t_r + t_f)·f_sw | ½·25.2·80·20 ns·48 k = **0.97 W** |
| Reverse recovery | Q_rr·V·f_sw | 30 nC·25.2·48 k = **0.036 W** |
| Coss charge | ½·Q_oss·V·f_sw | **0.015 W** |
| Dead-time diode conduction | V_f·I·2·t_dt·f_sw | 0.8·80·200 ns·48 k = **0.61 W** |
| Gate drive (6 FETs, dissipated in driver/R_g) | 6·Q_g·V_gs·f_sw | **0.115 W** |
| **Total switching-related** | | **≈ 1.75 W** |

Only one leg is PWM-switched at a time in 6-step, so the overlap term is counted once.

Takeaways:

- At 48 kHz, conduction dominates switching loss by about 17:1.
- The 48 kHz choice is justified for ripple and acoustic reasons, not for efficiency.
- Dead time is the largest controllable switching term. Reducing it from 100 ns to 50 ns saves 0.3 W per motor.

| Source | Per motor | ×4 motors |

| FET conduction | 29.1 W | 116.4 W |
| Switching + dead time | 1.75 W | 7.0 W |
| Bus/phase copper | ~8 W | ~33 W |
