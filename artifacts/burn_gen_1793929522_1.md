# STM32H743 Nano AIO with 4-in-1 ESC (Job #7): Architecture, Loss Model, DShot Allocation, Netlists

## 0. Architectural Corrections

The model below depends on three points:

1. **The STM32H743 does not commutate the motors.** ArduPilot runs on the H743 as the flight controller. A 4-in-1 ESC needs its own commutation MCU(s), here an AT32F421 running AM32 (one per motor) or a single STM32G071-class part. The H743 sends **bidirectional DShot600** to those MCUs. The **48 kHz** figure is the ESC MCU's PWM carrier, not an H743 timer.
2. **"80 A burst" on a nano board is a transient rating, not a continuous one.** Section 3 shows the board's thermal mass gives about **2–3 s** at 4×80 A before limits are reached. Sustained capability is about **25–30 A/motor**.
3. **4 × 80 A = 320 A from the battery.** At 6S that is roughly 8 kW. The XT60 connector, battery and pigtail cannot support it. This sets the system-level burst limit regardless of what the FETs can do.

## 1. Conduction Loss Model (80 A Burst)

### 1.1 Temperature-dependent on-resistance

$$R_{ds}(T_j) = R_{ds,25}\,[1+\alpha (T_j-25)]$$

At T_j = 125 °C: R_ds = 1.0 × (1 + 0.0045 × 100) = **1.45 mΩ**.

### 1.2 Commutation geometry

The model assumes 6-step trapezoidal drive with complementary (synchronous) PWM:

- At any instant, two phase legs conduct the phase current I: one high-side path and one low-side path.
- In the PWM'd leg, the high-side FET conducts for D and the low-side FET (synchronous rectification) for 1−D. The sum is always one R_ds in the path.

Per motor, conduction loss is therefore independent of duty cycle:

$$P_{cond,motor} = 2\,I^2\,R_{ds}(T_j)$$

Per FET (averaged over an electrical cycle, 6 FETs):

$$P_{cond,FET} = \frac{I^2 R_{ds}}{3}$$

### 1.3 Numbers at 80 A

| | 25 °C | 125 °C |
|---|---|---|
| P_cond per motor | 2·6400·0.001 = **12.8 W** | 2·6400·0.00145 = **18.6 W** |
| P_cond per FET (avg) | 2.13 W | 3.09 W |

### 1.4 Copper and PCB loss (often dominant on nano boards)

For a phase trace 8 mm long, 3 mm wide, on 2 layers of 2 oz copper (70 µm each):

$$R = \frac{\rho L}{A} = \frac{1.72\times10^{-8}\cdot 0.008}{0.003\cdot 140\times10^{-6}} \approx 0.33\,\text{m}\Omega$$

Two phase paths plus the shared bus (about 0.2 mΩ) give roughly **0.85 mΩ** per motor. That adds about 5.4 W at 80 A. Solder joints and pads add about 0.1–0.2 mΩ per joint.

**Conclusion:** use 4+ layers of copper on power nets, stitch vias generously (about 1 A per 0.3 mm via as a guideline), and place the FETs directly at the motor pads.

## 2. Switching Loss Model at 48 kHz

Only the PWM'd high-side FET hard-switches. The synchronous low-side FET turns on after dead time, so it is ZVS (zero-voltage switched) via its body diode.

### 2.1 Loss components

**(a) V–I overlap (hard switching):**
$$P_{ov} = \tfrac12 V_{bus} I (t_r + t_f) f_{sw} = \tfrac12(25.2)(80)(40\text{ns})(48\text{k}) = \mathbf{1.94\ W}$$

**(b) Reverse recovery of the opposing FET's body diode:**
$$P_{rr} = V_{bus} Q_{rr} f_{sw} = 25.2 \cdot 50\text{n} \cdot 48\text{k} = 0.060\ \text{W}$$
Strictly, I_rr also adds overlap loss. For a datasheet-specified Q_rr at di/dt, a margin of about 1.5× is recommended.

**(c) Output capacitance:**
$$P_{oss} = \tfrac12 C_{oss} V_{bus}^2 f_{sw} = \tfrac12(1.5\text{n})(635)(48\text{k}) = 0.023\ \text{W}$$

**(d) Dead-time body-diode conduction:**
$$P_{dt} = V_f\, I\, (2 t_{dt}) f_{sw} = 0.8\cdot 80\cdot 200\text{n}\cdot 48\text{k} = \mathbf{0.61\ W}$$

**(e) Gate drive** (dissipated mostly in the driver and gate resistor):
$$P_g = Q_g V_{gs} f_{sw} = 60\text{n}\cdot10\cdot48\text{k} = 0.029\ \text{W per switching FET}$$

### 2.2 Totals per motor at 80 A, 48 kHz

| Component | W |
|---|---|
| Overlap | 1.94 |
| Q_rr | 0.06 |
| C_oss | 0.02 |
| Dead time | 0.61 |
| Gate (2 FETs active) | 0.06 |
| **Switching total** | **≈ 2.7 W** |

### 2.3 Frequency trade-off

P_sw scales linearly with f_sw. At 24 kHz it is about 1.35 W; at 96 kHz about 5.4 W. At 48 kHz, conduction dominates (about 87%). Dropping to 24 kHz therefore buys little thermally but worsens current ripple on low-inductance motors. **48 kHz is a reasonable choice.**
