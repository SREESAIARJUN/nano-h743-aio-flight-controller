# STM32H743 AIO Flight Controller + 4-in-1 ESC: Reference Design Model (Job #68)

**Status:** Engineering reference. Every number below is derived from stated assumptions. Validate against the datasheets of the specific parts you select and against thermal-camera and bench measurements before release.

## 0. Architecture Clarifications

Three constraints shape the rest of this design.

1. **The H743 does not commutate the motors.** In every production "AIO" (all-in-one) board, the STM32H743 runs ArduPilot as the flight controller. Each motor (or all four) is driven by a dedicated ESC MCU running AM32 or BLHeli_32-class firmware, for example an AT32F421 or STM32G071. The H743 talks to that MCU over bidirectional DShot600. The 48 kHz figure is the ESC MCU's PWM frequency, not an H743 timer.
2. **80 A burst on a nano form factor is a seconds-scale rating, not a continuous one.** Section 3 shows that 4 × 80 A dissipates about 120 W in a board with roughly 4–5 J/K of useful heat capacity. That gives about 2 s from a warm start before the PCB exceeds 110 °C. Treat 80 A as a ≤1–2 s per-motor transient, with about 35–45 A continuous given airflow.
3. **The supply is assumed to be 6S** (V_bus max = 25.2 V, nominal 22.2 V), so the power stage uses 40 V-class MOSFETs.

## 2. Power-Stage Loss Model (per motor, 6 MOSFETs)

### 2.1 Conduction Loss

In 6-step commutation with synchronous rectification, the phase current always flows through exactly two conducting channels:

- During the on-time (fraction D), it flows through the high side of phase A and the low side of phase B.
- During the off-time (fraction 1 − D), it flows through the synchronous low side of phase A and the low side of phase B.

The duty-cycle terms therefore cancel:

$$P_{cond} = I_{pk}^2 \cdot R_{ds,hot}\,[D + (1-D)] + I_{pk}^2 \cdot R_{ds,hot} = 2\,I_{pk}^2\,R_{ds,hot}$$

$$P_{cond} = 2 \times 80^2 \times 2.1\times10^{-3} = \mathbf{26.9\ W}$$

Including the trace and copper path from the battery pad to the phase pad (about 0.3 mΩ round trip in 2 oz copper) adds 80² × 0.3 mΩ ≈ 1.9 W.

### 2.2 Hard-Switching (V·I Overlap) Loss

Only the PWM-ing leg hard-switches, once on and once off per period:

$$P_{sw} = \tfrac{1}{2}\, V_{bus}\, I_{pk}\,(t_r + t_f)\, f_{sw}$$

$$P_{sw} = 0.5 \times 25.2 \times 80 \times 20\times10^{-9} \times 48\,000 = \mathbf{0.97\ W}$$

### 2.3 Output-Capacitance (Coss) Loss

$$P_{oss} = Q_{oss}\, V_{bus}\, f_{sw} = 30\,\text{nC} \times 25.2 \times 48\,000 = \mathbf{0.036\ W}$$

### 2.4 Dead-Time Body-Diode Conduction

There are two dead-time intervals per PWM period:

$$P_{dt} = V_{SD}\, I_{pk}\, (2\, t_{dt})\, f_{sw} = 0.8 \times 80 \times 400\,\text{ns} \times 48\,000 = \mathbf{1.23\ W}$$

Reverse-recovery loss (Q_rr) is of the same order. Assuming Q_rr ≈ 50 nC:

$$P_{rr} \approx Q_{rr}\,V_{bus}\,f_{sw} = 50\,\text{nC} \times 25.2 \times 48\,000 \approx \mathbf{0.06\ W}$$

### 2.5 Gate-Drive Loss

Two FETs toggle at f_sw (the complementary pair):

$$P_g = 2\, Q_g\, V_{drv}\, f_{sw} = 2 \times 40\,\text{nC} \times 10 \times 48\,000 = \mathbf{0.038\ W}$$

This loss is dissipated mostly in the gate driver and gate resistors, not in the FETs.

### 2.6 Per-Motor and Board Totals at 80 A

| Loss term | W / motor |
|---|---|
| Conduction (FETs) | 26.9 |
| Copper path | 1.9 |
| Switching overlap | 0.97 |
| Dead-time diode | 1.23 |
| Coss + Qrr | 0.10 |
| Gate drive | 0.04 |
| **Total** | **≈ 31.1 W** |
| **4 motors** | **≈ 124 W** |

**Key insight:** at 48 kHz, conduction dominates (about 86 %). Dropping f_sw to 24 kHz saves only about 1.1 W per motor. Halving R_ds(on), for example by paralleling FETs or using larger packages, saves about 13 W per motor. The optimization lever is R_ds(on), not frequency.

### 2.7 Per-FET Distribution and Junction Rise

Over one electrical revolution, each phase leg carries current for 2/3 of the cycle. The worst-case single FET is the low side that is both commutating and synchronous-rectifying:

$$P_{FET,max} \approx I^2 R_{ds,hot} \cdot \tfrac{2}{3} + P_{sw} \approx 6400 \times 2.1\text{m} \times 0.667 + 1.0 \approx 10\ \text{W}$$

$$\Delta T_{j-c} = 10 \times 1.5 = 15\ \text{K (steady state)}$$

With Z_θJC at 10 ms ≈ 0.4 K/W, the rise is only about 4 K. The junction is therefore not the burst limiter; the PCB thermal mass is.

### 2.8 Iterating R_ds(on) Against Temperature

R_ds(on) rises with temperature, which raises loss, which raises temperature. The fixed-point form is:

$$R(T) = R_{25}\,[1 + \alpha (T - 25)],\quad \alpha \approx 0.005\ /K$$

Solving with T_j = T_pcb + ΔT_jc converges within 2–3 iterations. At T_pcb = 100 °C, R ≈ 1.4 mΩ × 1.375 = 1.93 mΩ. The 2.1 mΩ figure used above is therefore slightly conservative.
