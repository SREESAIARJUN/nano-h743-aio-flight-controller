# STM32H743 AIO Nano FC + 4-in-1 ESC (Job #46): Power-Stage Loss Model, Thermal Analysis, and Bidirectional DShot600 Timer/DMA Allocation

**Classification:** Reference-design analysis. All component values are stated assumptions and must be replaced with your selected parts' datasheet values before layout freeze.

---

## 0. Architectural Clarifications and Executive Findings

**Topology.** The STM32H743 does not commutate the MOSFETs. A credible design has two tiers:

- **FC tier:** STM32H743 running ArduPilot. It generates bidirectional DShot600 on four timer channels.
- **ESC tier:** four dedicated ESC MCUs, one per motor, typically STM32G071 or AT32F421 running AM32 or BLHeli_32. Each MCU drives a 3-phase gate driver and six N-FETs at 48 kHz PWM.

**Key findings:**

| # | Finding | Severity |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | 80 A per channel on a 20×20 mm nano board dissipates about 90 W total, and the board heats at about 15 K/s even before steady-state limits. Burst duration must be capped at about 3–4 s, or the board must grow to 30.5×30.5 mm. | **Critical** |
| F2 | Conduction loss dominates (about 85%). FET R_DS(on) at temperature is the primary design lever. Switching frequency is secondary. | High |
| F3 | 48 kHz is viable. Dead-time body-diode conduction (about 1.2 W/ch) is the largest switching-related term, not V·I overlap. | Medium |
| F4 | Bidirectional DShot on the H743 is straightforward because DMAMUX routes any request to any stream. The real constraints are timer grouping (one rate per timer) and stream budget. | Low |

**Modulation:** 6-step trapezoidal with complementary (synchronous) PWM. At any instant, current flows through exactly one high-side and one low-side path. During PWM-off, the synchronous FET replaces the body diode except during dead time.

## 2. Conduction Loss Model

### 2.1 General form

In the synchronous 6-step scheme, phase current always traverses two conducting FET channels:

$$
P_{cond} = I_{ph,rms}^2 \cdot 2R_{DS(on)}(T_j)
$$

For trapezoidal drive with low current ripple, I_rms ≈ I_ph. The inductor-ripple correction is:

$$
I_{rms}^2 = I_{ph}^2 + \frac{\Delta I^2}{12}, \qquad \Delta I = \frac{(V_{bus}-E)\,D}{L_{ph}\,f_{sw}}
$$

For a typical motor with L_ph ≈ 15 µH, ΔI ≈ 6–8 A. That adds less than 0.1% and is neglected.

### 2.2 Temperature-coupled solution

Because R_DS(on) depends on T_j, and T_j depends on P, the model must be solved iteratively:

$$
T_j = T_{pcb} + P_{FET}\cdot R_{\theta JC}, \quad R(T_j)=R_{25}[1+\alpha(T_j-25)]
$$

**Evaluated at T_j ≈ 136 °C:** R = 1.0 × (1 + 0.0045 × 111) ≈ **1.50 mΩ**.

$$
P_{cond} = 80^2 \times 2 \times 1.5\text{ mΩ} = 6400 \times 0.003 = \mathbf{19.2\ W/channel}
$$

- **Per conducting FET:** 9.6 W while conducting.
- **Per FET, time-averaged:** each FET conducts 1/3 of the electrical cycle in 6-step, so the average is about 3.2 W. However, the junction ripples toward the instantaneous value at low eRPM. Design to the instantaneous value for the stall and launch case.

## 3. Switching Loss Model at 48 kHz

Only the PWM-switched leg incurs these losses. Each term below is per channel.

### 3.1 V·I overlap (hard switching)

$$
P_{sw} = \tfrac12 V_{bus} I_{ph}(t_r+t_f) f_{sw} = 0.5 \times 25.2 \times 80 \times 40\text{ ns} \times 48\text{ kHz} = \mathbf{1.94\ W}
$$

### 3.2 Output-capacitance loss

$$
P_{oss} = \tfrac12 C_{oss(er)} V_{bus}^2 f_{sw} = 0.5 \times 1\text{ nF} \times 635 \times 48\text{ kHz} = \mathbf{0.015\ W}
$$

### 3.3 Reverse recovery

$$
P_{rr} = Q_{rr} V_{bus} f_{sw} = 30\text{ nC} \times 25.2 \times 48\text{ kHz} = \mathbf{0.036\ W}
$$

The Q_rr-induced turn-on current spike also adds to P_sw. Budget an additional +10–20% to 3.1 if your FET has a snappy body diode.

### 3.4 Dead-time body-diode conduction

$$
P_{dt} = V_{SD} I_{ph} \cdot 2t_{dt} \cdot f_{sw} = 0.8 \times 80 \times 400\text{ ns} \times 48\text{ kHz} = \mathbf{1.23\ W}
$$

This is the largest switching-related term. Reducing dead time from 200 ns to 100 ns saves about 0.6 W/ch. Do this only after verifying there is no shoot-through, using double-pulse testing with the actual driver and layout.

### 3.5 Gate drive

$$
P_g = 2\,Q_g V_{GS} f_{sw} = 2 \times 40\text{ nC} \times 10 \times 48\text{ kHz} = \mathbf{0.038\ W}
$$

This loss is dissipated in the driver and gate resistors, not the FET.

### 3.6 Frequency sensitivity

$$
\frac{\partial P_{total}}{\partial f_{sw}} \approx \frac{1.94+1.23+0.05+0.04}{48\text{ kHz}} \approx 68\ \text{µW/Hz}
$$

Dropping to 24 kHz saves about 1.6 W/ch, roughly 7% of total. Switching frequency is not the main lever. R_DS(on) is.

|---|---|---|---|
| Conduction | 19.20 | 76.8 | 85.3% |
| V·I switching | 1.94 | 7.8 | 8.6% |
| Dead-time diode | 1.23 | 4.9 | 5.5% |
| Q_rr + C_oss | 0.05 | 0.2 | 0.2% |
| Gate drive | 0.04 | 0.2 | 0.2% |
| **Total FET stage** | **22.5** | **≈ 90 W** | |
| Shunts, copper, connectors | about 2–4 | about 8–16 | See §5.3 |

**Efficiency check:** input power at 80 A × 25.2 V × average duty is at most 2 kW per channel. A 22.5 W loss is above 98% efficiency electrically. The problem is purely thermal density.

## 5. Thermal Model

### 5.1 Network (per FET, lumped)

```
Tj ──[RθJC 3.0]── Tcase ──[RθCS ~0.5 (solder)]── Tpcb ──[RθPCB-A]── Tambient
                                                   │
                                                 [C_board]
```

### 5.2 Junction rise above PCB (instantaneous conduction)

$$
\Delta T_{JC} = 9.6\text{ W}\times(3.0+0.5) \approx \mathbf{34\ K}
$$

Add the switching share for the PWM FET (+3.2 W × 3.5 ≈ 11 K). The total junction-to-board rise is about **45 K**.
