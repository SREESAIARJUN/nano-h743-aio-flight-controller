**Architecture.** The STM32H743 is the flight controller MCU. It generates bidirectional DShot600. The 4-in-1 ESC needs its own MCU (e.g., AT32F421 or STM32G071 running AM32/BLHeli_32) to do commutation and the 48 kHz PWM. The H743 does not drive gates directly. The models below cover both sides.

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|

**Feasibility flag up front.** "80 A burst" on a nano (20×20 mm) 4-in-1 is thermally marginal. The analysis below shows it is valid for roughly 1–3 s, not as a sustained rating. Treat it as a short peak, not a cruise rating.

## 1. Conduction Loss Model

### 1.1 Current path

In six-step (trapezoidal) commutation with synchronous rectification, phase current always flows through exactly **two** conducting channels:
- During the ON part of the cycle: the high-side (HS) of phase A and the low-side (LS) of phase B.
- During the OFF part: the synchronous LS of phase A and the LS of phase B.

Because of this, duty cycle cancels out of the conduction term:

$$P_{cond,motor} = 2\, I_{ph}^2\, R_{ds(on)}(T_j) \quad\text{where}\quad R_{ds(on)}(T_j)=R_{25}\left[1+\alpha (T_j-25)\right]$$

With α ≈ 0.0059 /K, this gives k_T ≈ 1.5 at T_j ≈ 110 °C.

### 1.2 Numbers at 80 A

$$P_{cond} = 2 \cdot 80^2 \cdot 1.5\,\text{m}\Omega = \mathbf{19.2\ W\ per\ motor}$$

**Per-FET distribution.** Each phase conducts for 2/3 of the electrical cycle. Averaged over the cycle, the 6 FETs of one motor share 19.2 W, so about **3.2 W per FET average**. The instantaneous peak is about 9.6 W in a conducting FET.

**PCB copper (often the overlooked term).** Use R = ρ·L/(W·t), with ρ_Cu = 1.72×10⁻⁸ Ω·m, raised about 30 % when hot. For a 2 oz (70 µm) pour, 8 mm long and 4 mm wide:

$$R \approx \frac{1.72\times10^{-8}\cdot 0.008}{0.004\cdot 70\times10^{-6}}\cdot 1.3 \approx 0.64\ \text{m}\Omega$$

The current passes through roughly 2–3 such segments plus vias, about 1.5 mΩ total:

$$P_{Cu} = 80^2\cdot 1.5\,\text{m}\Omega \approx \mathbf{9.6\ W\ per\ motor}$$

Copper loss is comparable to FET loss. Use 4–6 layers with stacked 2 oz power layers.

## 2. Switching Losses at 48 kHz

Per PWM period, one FET per active phase pair hard-switches. The sync FET soft-switches through its body diode.

### 2.1 Voltage–current overlap (V–I crossover)

The switching interval is set by the Miller plateau. With V_plateau ≈ 4.5 V and R_g,total ≈ 4 Ω:

$$t_{sw} \approx \frac{Q_{gd}}{I_{g}} ;\quad t_r\approx \frac{12\,\text{nC}}{(10-4.5)/4}\approx 8.7\ \text{ns},\quad t_f\approx\frac{12\,\text{nC}}{4.5/4}\approx 10.7\ \text{ns}$$

$$P_{VI} = \tfrac12 V_{bus} I (t_r+t_f) f_{sw} = \tfrac12(25.2)(80)(19.4\,\text{ns})(48\,\text{kHz}) = \mathbf{0.94\ W}$$

### 2.2 Dead-time body-diode conduction

$$P_{dt} = V_{SD}\, I\, (2 t_{dt}) f_{sw} = 0.8\cdot80\cdot200\,\text{ns}\cdot48\,\text{kHz} = \mathbf{0.61\ W}$$

### 2.3 Reverse recovery

$$P_{rr} = Q_{rr} V_{bus} f_{sw} = 50\,\text{nC}\cdot25.2\cdot48\,\text{k} = \mathbf{0.06\ W}$$

Note that Q_rr grows with di/dt, so check the datasheet at your di/dt.

### 2.4 Output capacitance

$$P_{oss} = \tfrac12 C_{oss,eq} V^2 f_{sw} = \tfrac12(1.5\,\text{nF})(635)(48\,\text{k}) = \mathbf{0.02\ W}$$

### 2.5 Gate drive

This loss lands in the driver and gate resistors, not the FET:

$$P_g = 2\, Q_g V_{GS} f_{sw} = 2\cdot60\,\text{nC}\cdot10\cdot48\,\text{k} = \mathbf{0.06\ W\ per\ motor}$$

### 2.6 Switching total

**About 1.7 W per motor.** At 48 kHz, switching loss is under 10 % of conduction loss. This means the burst limit is set by R_ds(on) and copper, not by f_sw.

## 3. Loss Budget and Thermal Model

### 3.1 Loss budget at 80 A

| Term | Per motor | ×4 motors |
|---|---|---|
| FET conduction | 19.2 W | 76.8 W |
| PCB copper | 9.6 W | 38.4 W |
| Switching (all) | 1.7 W | 6.8 W |
| Bulk cap ESR (see 3.4) | ~0.5 W | 2 W |
| **Total** | **~31 W** | **~124 W** |

The ESC efficiency stays high: 124 W lost versus about 4 × 80 × 25.2 × D of shaft power. The problem is power **density**, not efficiency.

### 3.2 Junction-to-board rise (fast term)

For a conducting FET dissipating 9.6 W instantaneous:

$$\Delta T_{JC} = 9.6 \cdot 1.2 \approx 11.5\ \text{K}$$

The thermal time constant here is milliseconds, so this rise is effectively immediate.
